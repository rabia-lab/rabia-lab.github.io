Biography

My interest in agriculture began in Egypt, where I studied agricultural sciences and earned my first master's degree in soil and water sciences. The turning point came when I traveled to Italy for my second master's degree in remote sensing and GIS. I immediately fell in love with the field because I saw how these technologies could connect with my knowledge of soil, water, and agricultural systems to help solve real problems for farmers. My agricultural background helps me understand why a problem occurs, while remote sensing and GIS help me see where, when, and how it develops. That combination convinced me to pursue precision agriculture, and it continues to shape my work with UAVs, artificial intelligence, and robotics.

I established the lab with a practical mission: to generate innovative science and develop tools that help plant breeders strengthen their programs and farmers improve their operations, producing more with less, increasing profitability, and using resources sustainably. We can now collect enormous amounts of agricultural data from satellites, drones, sensors, and machinery, but translating that data into useful decisions remains a major challenge. Our lab brings together agricultural science, engineering, remote sensing, artificial intelligence, and automation to bridge that gap. For breeders, this means better crop evaluation and high-throughput phenotyping; for farmers, it means earlier problem detection, more precise input management, and intelligent tools that work under real field conditions. I want our success measured by both scientific advances and practical impact.

Leading the lab has changed the way I approach research. I began with a strong focus on soil science, remote sensing, and GIS; now I think more about how agronomy, engineering, computer science, and robotics can work together to solve problems no single discipline can address alone. My experiences in Egypt, Italy, and the United States have reinforced that perspective. Leading the lab has also strengthened my commitment to collaboration, mentorship, and translating research beyond scientific publications. Seeing students grow into confident researchers is as rewarding as a successful experiment. The experience continually reminds me to ask not only what we can discover, but also what we can build, who can benefit, and how we can prepare the next generation of agricultural innovators.

My primary field is precision agriculture, integrating remote sensing, GIS, artificial intelligence, machine learning, robotics, and autonomous systems. The question that drives my work is: How can we turn changing field conditions and complex data into timely, precise decisions that improve productivity and profitability while reducing environmental impacts? We use satellite and UAV imagery to monitor crop stress and variability, develop dynamic management zones for site-specific inputs, and apply computer vision to detect weeds and diseases and enable targeted robotic or laser-based management. We also study soil health and crop response. My long-term goal is to move from simply observing fields toward systems that can predict, recommend, and eventually perform precise management actions.

Over the next few years, I hope the lab becomes a leading contributor to agricultural intelligence for North Dakota and the Northern Great Plains. Our priorities include near-real-time crop monitoring, dynamic management zones, AI-powered decision support, autonomous weed management, and technologies that improve soil health and resource efficiency. I also want to expand our work in agricultural robotics and explore digital twins, sensor fusion, explainable AI, and edge computing where they can address real agricultural needs. Equally important, I want to strengthen partnerships with growers, plant breeders, industry, and other researchers so our technologies can be tested, improved, and adopted in the field. I hope the lab produces useful tools while preparing students to lead the next generation of agricultural innovation.

I expect students and researchers to bring curiosity, integrity, responsibility, and a willingness to learn. That means asking strong questions, designing careful experiments, handling data honestly, taking ownership of projects, and communicating results clearly. I also value teamwork and mutual respect, because precision agriculture depends on people with different skills learning from one another. Research rarely follows a straight path, so I encourage thoughtful risks, resilience, and learning from unexpected results. I want everyone to connect their daily work, whether an AI model, a drone mission, or a field trial, to a real agricultural need. Above all, I aim to create a supportive environment where students grow into independent, creative, and responsible researchers.

What keeps me motivated is the belief that science and technology should improve people's lives and make agriculture more sustainable. Agriculture faces difficult challenges involving food production, water, soil health, climate variability, and resource efficiency, and new technologies offer opportunities to address them. But I have learned that technology is valuable only when we understand the problem and apply the right tools in a meaningful way. I am energized by turning questions into discoveries and discoveries into practical solutions that breeders and farmers can use. Mentoring young researchers is another important motivation because their ideas extend our work's impact. For me, research remains a continuing journey of curiosity, collaboration, discovery, and service.
